# Codebook policy v2.2

Changelog v2.2 (2026-11-10, the author's coding decisions 5 to 10; every v2.1 rule stands except where a line below changes it):
- R5 (new field RULE_BREAKING; BEHAVIOUR; OUTSIDE_ATTACKER): a participant that breaks the rules or terms of its own exchange for its own or its principal's gain (licence violation, unauthorized derivatives of a model it received, a coalition of users stripping a fingerprint, cheating a counterparty, substituting a cheaper model than the one paid for) is recorded in RULE_BREAKING, is rational, and is not an outside attacker; strategic behaviour within the rules is rule_breaking no; outside attackers (poisoning, backdoors, adversarial examples, jailbreaks, extraction or theft by outsiders, privacy attacks) stay in OUTSIDE_ATTACKER (worked examples added).
- R6 (POLICY_SETTERS, BEHAVIOUR): players modelled as rational who all run the paper's prescribed algorithm are one, and BEHAVIOUR records rational (worked example added).
- R7 (PRINCIPALS): ad hoc teamwork and human-AI partners on one shared task reward serve one principal (worked example added).
- R9 (PRINCIPALS): a general assistant deployed to many users whose beliefs or data it absorbs serves those users as several principals (worked example added).
- R10 (POLICY_SETTERS, BEHAVIOUR): the word "mechanism" alone implies nothing about strategic agents; code what the abstract states (worked example added).
- Output: new key "rule_breaking" after "behaviour_quote".

Changelog v2.1 (2026-11-10, after the two-coder pilot of v2; clarifications and worked examples only, every v2 rule stands):
- C1 (all fields; attack and defence papers): an outside attacker is recorded in OUTSIDE_ATTACKER only and never counted as a policy setter, a principal or an interacting decision-maker; the other fields describe the setting the attack targets or the defence protects.
- C2 (data subjects): people or populations that respond to a model only through a change in the data (performative prediction with a distribution map, users in a feedback or lock-in loop) are not decision-makers; neither is an evolving natural process called an opponent.
- C3 (POLICY_SETTERS): an author-run simulation whose agents stand for real parties is still one (worked example added).
- C4 (POLICY_SETTERS): players called rational or strategic who all run the paper's algorithm are one, and a game the method uses as its own device is prescribed (worked examples added).
- C5 (POLICY_SETTERS): an incentive or attribution method that models no participant's choice is one (worked example added).
- C6 (POLICY_SETTERS): a secure-computation protocol prescribed for every party is one; a party the protocol does not bind is its own setter.
- C7 (POLICY_SETTERS): the owner of an audited, traced or tested model is a separate setter only when the method queries or runs that model.
- C8 (PRINCIPALS): models built by different parties serve several principals unless the abstract says otherwise; data subjects are principals only where the model serves them for their own use; agents standing for real parties count those parties.
- C9 (BEHAVIOUR): a counterparty that may cheat inside the exchange is rational unless the guarantee is stated against any strategy (byzantine).
- C10 (INTERACTING): agents that only supply preferences or answers to one mediator or learner, and tests or audits applied to another party's model, are not interacting.

A separate pass beside codebook v3.4, with its own questions and output. It records who sets the policy of each decision-maker, how many parties' interests the decision-makers serve, how the setting treats the participants' behaviour, whether a participant breaks the rules of its own exchange, whether decision-makers interact, whether the setting has an attacker from outside the exchange, and whether the method faces agents that other parties train. v2 (2026-11-10, after two audits of the v1 codes and the author's coding decisions) keeps v1's three fields, defines a policy setter by control with worked examples, and adds BEHAVIOUR, INTERACTING and OUTSIDE_ATTACKER; v2.2 adds RULE_BREAKING. Answer only the questions below; this codebook has no other fields.

Code each paper from its title and abstract only, reading the abstract to its end. Code the setting the paper's method or analysis is meant for (its intended use or real-world referent), even when the paper's own experiments simulate that setting; do not code settings mentioned only in passing (an example task, a motivation sentence). Code what the setting assumes, never what a party could physically do: a client that runs open code on its own machine could change that code, but if the paper models it running the protocol, it does not set its policy. A decision-maker is any learner, model, agent, player, person or organization that chooses actions in that setting. People whose choices the paper only observes, fits with a choice model, uses as training data or aggregates as preferences (data subjects, annotators, raters, users in logged data) are not decision-makers; a person is a decision-maker only when the paper models that person choosing actions in the setting (a strategic user, a bidder, a human player or teammate, a worker who decides whether to cheat). This holds also when the people respond to the model's outputs, so long as the paper models the response as a change in the data rather than as a choice: a population whose features or outcomes shift with the predictions through a distribution map (performative prediction), or users whose beliefs or clicks a model absorbs and feeds back to them in a feedback, echo-chamber or lock-in loop. A natural or evolving process (a virus, a disease, a price series) is part of the environment, not a decision-maker, also when the paper calls it an opponent or adversary. A paper in which one developer trains or runs one model is the default setting: policy_setters one, principals one, behaviour na, rule_breaking no, interacting no, outside_attacker no, partners_from_others no. Use na only where the abstract does not fix the answer, or where the paper has no decision-maker at all (for example a pure hypothesis test or a dataset with no learner).

Attack and defence papers: an outside attacker (as OUTSIDE_ATTACKER defines it) is recorded in OUTSIDE_ATTACKER only. It is never counted as a policy setter, a principal or an interacting decision-maker, whether the paper's method is the attack or the defence: code POLICY_SETTERS, PRINCIPALS, INTERACTING and PARTNERS_FROM_OTHERS for the setting that the attack targets or the defence protects, as if the attacker were absent. An attack on, or a defence of, a model that one party trains or deploys (adversarial examples, jailbreaks, membership inference or extraction against it, poisoning of its training data, adversarial training, certified or inference-time defences) is the default setting with outside_attacker yes: one, one, behaviour na, rule_breaking no, interacting no. A participant that breaks the rules or terms of its own exchange for its own or its principal's gain is not an outside attacker, also when the abstract calls it malicious or calls its breach an attack: it is recorded in RULE_BREAKING and counted as a participant in every other field. When the protected setting has several parties of its own (federated clients and a server, a model provider and the users it licenses a model to, data owners and a model trainer), code those parties as for any other setting. BEHAVIOUR counts the attacker only when the attacker is, or controls, one of the setting's participants (poisoning or Byzantine clients, an honest-but-curious server); an attacker that only manipulates a model's inputs, queries or training data leaves BEHAVIOUR as it would be without the attacker.

POLICY_SETTERS: in the setting the method or analysis is meant for, who sets the policy of each decision-maker? A party sets a decision-maker's policy if it trains, fine-tunes, prompts or programs it, or chooses which model or agent to deploy, including changing, swapping or retraining it between interactions. one: one party sets every decision-maker's policy. This includes a single model or learner; a team or population of agents that one developer trains; multi-agent learning in which the authors train, prompt or run every agent, whatever rewards, personas or objectives the agents are given; a simulated market or society whose every agent the designer programs; and a protocol or algorithm that the paper prescribes for every participant. several: at least two decision-makers have policies set by different parties, each setting its own: agents built, trained, prompted or deployed separately by different parties, people or organizations who choose their own actions, and participants that choose part of their behaviour for their own objective or bring a model that their owner trained outside the method. na: the abstract does not say who sets the decision-makers' policies, or there is no decision-maker. Decide by who sets each policy, never by the decision-makers' roles, names or interests, never by who holds the hardware or the data, and never by who designs their rewards, incentives or the rules they act under: a party that designs the rewards or the rules but leaves each decision-maker to choose its own actions does not set their policies. Data partition, heterogeneous architectures, exchange of outputs or distillation, asynchrony, partial participation and decentralization never make several setters by themselves, and choices that the method computes on the participants' behalf (coalitions, groupings, client selection) are prescribed. A cryptographic or secure-computation protocol (secure inference, multi-party computation, a proof protocol) whose steps the paper prescribes for every party is prescribed (one), even when the parties distrust each other; a party that the protocol does not bind and that may follow any strategy of its own (a party whose claims the protocol only checks, a provider that may cheat on what it proves) sets its own policy. The owner of a model that the method audits, traces, verifies or tests (for provenance, membership or fairness) is a separate setter only when the method queries, runs or interacts with that model in the setting; a procedure that the auditor runs only on its own data and models is one. Formal-model players, which no real party trains, are coded from the setting's assumption about them: players assumed to best respond, maximize their own payoffs or reach an equilibrium for their own payoffs code several, also when the paper computes, certifies or learns that equilibrium; players that all run an algorithm or dynamics that the paper prescribes code one, also when the paper models them as rational, each with its own utility (BEHAVIOUR then records rational). The word "mechanism" alone implies nothing about strategic agents: code what the abstract states about the agents (that they report, bid, misreport or act for their own utility), and where it states nothing about them, code POLICY_SETTERS and BEHAVIOUR as the rest of the abstract fixes them, or na. For real parties (organizations, people, deployed agents) behaviour never decides: code who sets the policy.

Worked examples for POLICY_SETTERS (invented settings):
- A federation of clinics trains one diagnostic model; every clinic runs the local update and the server runs the aggregation that the paper prescribes: one.
- The same federation, but each clinic decides whether to join, how much data or effort to contribute or what to report, for its own benefit, and the paper models that choice (free-riding, a payment or incentive mechanism, an equilibrium of contributions): several.
- The same federation, and the paper estimates each clinic's contribution so that a later reward can be fair, motivated by clinics' incentives to take part, but models no clinic's choice: one.
- Each participant brings a model that its owner trained outside the method (a pre-trained black-box expert, another company's detector) and the method combines their outputs: several.
- Clients with different architectures exchange logits for distillation, and the method prescribes every client's loss, temperature and schedule: one.
- Players in a game all run the paper's no-regret dynamics, self-play or independent policy gradient, and the paper studies where the play converges: one, also when the abstract says the players learn independently or without communication.
- Firms modelled as rational players, each with its own utility, all run the per-player update rule that the paper proposes, and the paper proves that the play reaches an equilibrium: one (BEHAVIOUR still records rational). The same holds when the method uses a game as its own device (a potential, matching or coalition game whose solution the method computes or runs for every participant).
- Commuters modelled as self-interested, each minimizing its own travel time, all run the route-update rule that the paper proposes, and the paper shows that traffic settles at an equilibrium: one, behaviour rational, interacting yes.
- Bidders in an auction maximize their own utilities, and the paper proves the complexity of, computes or learns the auction's equilibrium: several.
- A paper designs a "mechanism" that splits a shared compute budget among teams and proves an approximation ratio, and the abstract never says that the teams report, bid or choose anything: the word decides nothing, so policy_setters na and behaviour na unless the rest of the abstract fixes them; if the abstract says the teams may misreport their demands for a larger share, several and behaviour rational.
- The authors run several language-model agents, or train several reinforcement-learning agents, that negotiate, debate or trade, each with its own reward or persona: one.
- The authors train every agent of a simulated electricity market, each agent standing for a real utility company with its own interests, to study which market rules the real companies should adopt: one, because the authors set every agent's policy, not the companies the agents stand for (PRINCIPALS several, INTERACTING yes).
- Agents of different organizations cooperate on a task through a protocol such as MCP or A2A, each organization prompting and deploying its own agent: several, even though they cooperate.
- A method for agents whose owners may swap, update or retrain them between rounds of interaction: several.
- A recommender learns from the click logs of many users whom the paper models only through a choice model: the users are data, not decision-makers, so one; if the paper models users or content creators choosing strategically in response to the system, several.
- A lender's credit model changes the distribution of applicants' features, which the paper models through a distribution map, or a news recommender's users drift toward the opinions it shows them in a feedback loop: one (the people are data); if the applicants are modelled as choosing which features to change for their own benefit (strategic classification), several.
- A transfer attack crafts adversarial images on a surrogate model to fool another party's classifier, or a defence makes one model robust to such attacks: one, one, behaviour na, rule_breaking no, interacting no, outside_attacker yes. An honest-but-curious server reconstructs clients' data from their updates in protocol-fixed federated learning: one, principals several, behaviour byzantine, rule_breaking no, interacting no, outside_attacker yes.

PRINCIPALS: how many distinct parties' interests do the decision-makers serve in that setting? one: all decision-makers serve one party: one user, one organization, or one team objective. A team trained by one developer for one shared reward serves one principal, and so do agents with separate rewards that one developer designs to serve its own goal. several: the decision-makers serve at least two distinct parties, whether those parties' interests are aligned or conflicting. A single learner that serves several data holders or user groups with their own interests is several: clinics in a cross-silo federation, clients that each receive a personalized model, fairness across clients, or one model that aggregates the different preferences of several user or annotator groups; the team rule does not apply to it, because the learner serves the data holders, not the developer of the protocol. na: the abstract does not say whose interests the decision-makers serve, or there is no decision-maker. Count parties, not agents: many agents serving one party are one principal. Models or agents that different parties build or deploy serve at least those parties: several, unless the abstract says they all serve one party. Data subjects are not principals because a model is fitted to them: per-person models built to study or predict individuals serve the analyst (one); people are principals only where the model serves them for their own use (users of a deployed assistant, clients that receive personalized models). Agents that the authors run but that stand for distinct real parties (regions, firms, countries) serve those parties (several); agents that the authors give opposing roles to test their own method or system (a red team and a blue team, a proposer and a critic) serve the authors' goal (one). Ad hoc teamwork and human-AI partners working toward one shared task reward serve one principal, also when the agent adapts to the partner's own preferences or conventions. A general assistant deployed to many users whose beliefs or data it absorbs serves those users: several (a single learner serving several parties).

Worked examples for PRINCIPALS (invented settings):
- A kitchen robot learns to cook with a human partner it has never met; both are rewarded by the same meal score, and the robot also adapts to how the partner likes to split the chores: one.
- A writing assistant is deployed to millions of users and is retrained on their conversations, so their opinions flow into the next version and back to them: several (the users), with policy_setters one and interacting no.

BEHAVIOUR: in the setting the paper models, how does it treat the participants (the decision-makers that act in the setting besides, or together with, the paper's own method) relative to the rules, protocol, algorithm or mechanism they act under? The terms follow distributed algorithmic mechanism design, where obedient nodes "simply do what they are programmed to do", strategic nodes are "the selfish agents of game theory", and faulty and adversarial nodes range from honest-but-curious to Byzantine. obedient: every participant follows a procedure, protocol or algorithm that the paper prescribes, and the paper models no choice to depart from it (federated clients running the protocol, players all running the paper's learning dynamics, agents following the workflow the authors give them, people following instructions). rational: at least one participant chooses or learns its actions for its own or its principal's objective where the rules cannot bind it: it may misreport, withhold, refuse to take part, free-ride, bid, best respond, reach an equilibrium, deviate from the terms of an exchange for its own gain, or run a learning algorithm that the paper does not prescribe (strategic users, bidders, selfish clients, equilibrium players, self-interested agents, other parties' learners, an opponent of unknown policy that plays for its own payoff). Participants that collude or form coalitions for their own gain stay rational: a coalition of rational participants is rational, and only participants that deviate arbitrarily are byzantine. byzantine: at least one participant may malfunction, deviate arbitrarily, or act to harm others with goals that the paper does not model as its own utility (faulty or Byzantine nodes, malicious or poisoning clients, honest-but-curious parties that follow the protocol but try to learn what they should not, arbitrary or worst-case opponents), and no participant is modelled as rational. mixed: rational and byzantine participants both appear. not-stated: the setting has at least two decision-makers, but the abstract neither describes a procedure that they all run nor says how they act relative to the rules; cooperative partners of unknown policy that share the task's goal are not-stated unless the abstract models them departing from it. Code obedient only when the abstract describes the procedure that every participant runs and models no departure from it; never default to obedient. na: there is no decision-maker, or a single decision-maker acts with no other participant (the default setting; one model trained on several groups' data or preferences). A counterparty inside the exchange that may cheat, misreport or commit malfeasance on what it provides or proves is rational, unless the abstract states the guarantee against any or arbitrary strategy of that party (security or soundness that holds whatever that party does, a worst-case counterparty), which is byzantine. A participant that breaks the rules or terms of its own exchange for its own or its principal's gain (RULE_BREAKING yes) is rational, also when the abstract calls it malicious or calls its breach an attack; licence violation, unauthorized derivatives of a model it received, a coalition of users stripping a fingerprint, cheating a counterparty and substituting a cheaper model than the one paid for count as for its own gain unless the abstract states another aim, and the any-strategy guarantee above still codes byzantine. BEHAVIOUR never changes POLICY_SETTERS for real parties; it decides POLICY_SETTERS only for formal-model players, as stated there.

RULE_BREAKING: yes if the setting models at least one participant breaking the rules or terms of its own exchange (protocol, mechanism, contract, licence, terms of service) for its own or its principal's gain: using a model or data beyond the terms of its licence, creating unauthorized derivatives of a model it received, users forming a coalition to strip or evade a fingerprint or watermark of a model they received, cheating a counterparty on what it delivers, proves or reports (a provider serving a cheaper model than the one paid for, a platform answering an auditor differently from its users, a prover or service provider that may cheat, whatever BEHAVIOUR records for it), sending crafted updates in place of the prescribed ones for a competitive advantage, or faking activity to claim a larger payment. Code yes also when the paper's method only detects, proves, deters or adjudicates the breach (provenance tests, fingerprints, verifiable inference, manipulation-proof audits, zero-knowledge certificates): the participant it guards against is modelled. no if the participants are modelled acting within the rules, including strategically: following the protocol, or choosing for their own benefit among the actions the rules leave to them (bidding, reporting a private type or valuation to a mechanism, truthfully or not, best responding, choosing whether to join, how much to contribute or which features to change, deceiving where the game's rules allow it); and no if the setting has no such participant, including a single decision-maker (the default setting) and a setting whose only adversary is an outside attacker (OUTSIDE_ATTACKER yes), which is never a rule-breaking participant. na if there is no decision-maker.

INTERACTING: yes if at least two decision-makers interact in the setting, each acting on the other's actions or on outcomes that the other's actions shape: players in a game, bidders and sellers in a market or auction, negotiating or debating agents, agents in multi-agent reinforcement learning, a learner and the strategic users who respond to it, agents of different owners exchanging messages or coordinating on a task. no for a single learner, model or algorithm, however many parties it serves or whose data it uses: protocol-fixed federated learning that trains one model or personalized models by one protocol, preference aggregation or pluralistic alignment inside one model, a mixture of experts or a router inside one system, several independently built models evaluated side by side, agents whose only role is to supply preferences, utilities or answers to queries to one mediator, mechanism or learner that decides for them (when the paper does not model them choosing what to supply), and a test, audit, trace or watermark check that one party applies to another party's model or released output. na if there is no decision-maker.

OUTSIDE_ATTACKER: yes if the setting includes an adversary whose aim is to harm, compromise or extract from a victim (a model, its owner, its users or the other participants) by acting outside the terms of any exchange it takes part in, rather than breaking the terms of its own exchange for its own gain: data or model poisoning, backdoors, adversarial examples, jailbreaks and prompt injection, model or data extraction and model theft by outsiders, membership inference, gradient inversion and other privacy attacks, including honest-but-curious parties whose threat is what they learn. no otherwise, including a participant that deviates inside an exchange for its own or its principal's gain, within the rules (misreporting, free-riding) or breaking them (RULE_BREAKING yes: substituting a cheaper model than the one paid for, using a model or data beyond the terms of its licence, creating unauthorized derivatives of a model it received, a coalition of users stripping a fingerprint, cheating a counterparty, sending crafted updates for a competitive advantage), also when the abstract calls it malicious or an attack, participants that collude for their own gain, worst-case or adversarial opponents in a game or an online-learning analysis, and robustness to noise or distribution shift with no attacker modelled.

Worked examples for RULE_BREAKING and OUTSIDE_ATTACKER (invented settings):
- A company licenses its speech model to app developers, and the paper tests whether a suspect app runs an unlicensed fine-tune of it: policy_setters several, principals several, behaviour rational, rule_breaking yes, outside_attacker no.
- A cloud service sells access to a named model, and the paper lets buyers check from the outputs that the service has not switched to a cheaper model: several, several, behaviour rational, rule_breaking yes, outside_attacker no.
- Customers who each received a fingerprinted copy of a model pool their copies to erase the fingerprint, and the paper's fingerprints survive the pooling: several, several, behaviour rational, rule_breaking yes, outside_attacker no.
- Suppliers in a procurement auction may misstate their costs, and the paper designs an auction in which truthful bids are optimal: several, several, behaviour rational, rule_breaking no, outside_attacker no.
- An outsider plants poisoned reviews in the public data that a recommender is trained on, or jailbreaks a deployed chatbot: outside_attacker yes, rule_breaking no, and the other fields describe the attacked setting.

PARTNERS_FROM_OTHERS: yes if the method is built to act with or against agents that other parties train or run and that it has not seen in training; no otherwise; na if there is no decision-maker.

Output one JSON object per paper with exactly these keys: "id", "policy_setters", "policy_quote", "principals", "principals_quote", "behaviour", "behaviour_quote", "rule_breaking", "interacting", "outside_attacker", "partners_from_others". Values: policy_setters one / several / na; principals one / several / na; behaviour obedient / rational / byzantine / mixed / not-stated / na; rule_breaking yes / no / na; interacting yes / no / na; outside_attacker yes / no; partners_from_others yes / no / na. "policy_quote" is the shortest verbatim phrase from the title or abstract that decided POLICY_SETTERS, "principals_quote" the shortest verbatim phrase that decided PRINCIPALS, and "behaviour_quote" the shortest verbatim phrase that decided BEHAVIOUR; each is an empty string if no phrase decided it.
