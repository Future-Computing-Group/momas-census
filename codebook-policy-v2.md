# Codebook policy v2

A separate pass beside codebook v3.4, with its own questions and output. It records who sets the policy of each decision-maker, how many parties' interests the decision-makers serve, how the setting treats the participants' behaviour, whether decision-makers interact, whether the setting has an attacker from outside the exchange, and whether the method faces agents that other parties train. v2 (2026-11-10, after two audits of the v1 codes and the author's coding decisions) keeps v1's three fields, defines a policy setter by control with worked examples, and adds BEHAVIOUR, INTERACTING and OUTSIDE_ATTACKER. Answer only the questions below; this codebook has no other fields.

Code each paper from its title and abstract only, reading the abstract to its end. Code the setting the paper's method or analysis is meant for (its intended use or real-world referent), even when the paper's own experiments simulate that setting; do not code settings mentioned only in passing (an example task, a motivation sentence). Code what the setting assumes, never what a party could physically do: a client that runs open code on its own machine could change that code, but if the paper models it running the protocol, it does not set its policy. A decision-maker is any learner, model, agent, player, person or organization that chooses actions in that setting. People whose choices the paper only observes, fits with a choice model, uses as training data or aggregates as preferences (data subjects, annotators, raters, users in logged data) are not decision-makers; a person is a decision-maker only when the paper models that person choosing actions in the setting (a strategic user, a bidder, a human player or teammate, a worker who decides whether to cheat). A paper in which one developer trains or runs one model is the default setting: policy_setters one, principals one, behaviour na, interacting no, outside_attacker no, partners_from_others no. Use na only where the abstract does not fix the answer, or where the paper has no decision-maker at all (for example a pure hypothesis test or a dataset with no learner).

POLICY_SETTERS: in the setting the method or analysis is meant for, who sets the policy of each decision-maker? A party sets a decision-maker's policy if it trains, fine-tunes, prompts or programs it, or chooses which model or agent to deploy, including changing, swapping or retraining it between interactions. one: one party sets every decision-maker's policy. This includes a single model or learner; a team or population of agents that one developer trains; multi-agent learning in which the authors train, prompt or run every agent, whatever rewards, personas or objectives the agents are given; a simulated market or society whose every agent the designer programs; and a protocol or algorithm that the paper prescribes for every participant. several: at least two decision-makers have policies set by different parties, each setting its own: agents built, trained, prompted or deployed separately by different parties, people or organizations who choose their own actions, and participants that choose part of their behaviour for their own objective or bring a model that their owner trained outside the method. na: the abstract does not say who sets the decision-makers' policies, or there is no decision-maker. Decide by who sets each policy, never by the decision-makers' roles, names or interests, never by who holds the hardware or the data, and never by who designs their rewards, incentives or the rules they act under: a party that designs the rewards or the rules but leaves each decision-maker to choose its own actions does not set their policies. Data partition, heterogeneous architectures, exchange of outputs or distillation, asynchrony, partial participation and decentralization never make several setters by themselves, and choices that the method computes on the participants' behalf (coalitions, groupings, client selection) are prescribed. Formal-model players, which no real party trains, are coded from the setting's assumption about them: players assumed to best respond, maximize their own payoffs or reach an equilibrium for their own payoffs code several, also when the paper computes, certifies or learns that equilibrium; players that all run an algorithm or dynamics that the paper prescribes code one. For real parties (organizations, people, deployed agents) behaviour never decides: code who sets the policy.

Worked examples for POLICY_SETTERS (invented settings):
- A federation of clinics trains one diagnostic model; every clinic runs the local update and the server runs the aggregation that the paper prescribes: one.
- The same federation, but each clinic decides whether to join, how much data or effort to contribute or what to report, for its own benefit, and the paper models that choice (free-riding, a payment or incentive mechanism, an equilibrium of contributions): several.
- Each participant brings a model that its owner trained outside the method (a pre-trained black-box expert, another company's detector) and the method combines their outputs: several.
- Clients with different architectures exchange logits for distillation, and the method prescribes every client's loss, temperature and schedule: one.
- Players in a game all run the paper's no-regret dynamics, self-play or independent policy gradient, and the paper studies where the play converges: one, also when the abstract says the players learn independently or without communication.
- Bidders in an auction maximize their own utilities, and the paper proves the complexity of, computes or learns the auction's equilibrium: several.
- The authors run several language-model agents, or train several reinforcement-learning agents, that negotiate, debate or trade, each with its own reward or persona: one.
- Agents of different organizations cooperate on a task through a protocol such as MCP or A2A, each organization prompting and deploying its own agent: several, even though they cooperate.
- A method for agents whose owners may swap, update or retrain them between rounds of interaction: several.
- A recommender learns from the click logs of many users whom the paper models only through a choice model: the users are data, not decision-makers, so one; if the paper models users or content creators choosing strategically in response to the system, several.

PRINCIPALS: how many distinct parties' interests do the decision-makers serve in that setting? one: all decision-makers serve one party: one user, one organization, or one team objective. A team trained by one developer for one shared reward serves one principal, and so do agents with separate rewards that one developer designs to serve its own goal. several: the decision-makers serve at least two distinct parties, whether those parties' interests are aligned or conflicting. A single learner that serves several data holders or user groups with their own interests is several: clinics in a cross-silo federation, clients that each receive a personalized model, fairness across clients, or one model that aggregates the different preferences of several user or annotator groups; the team rule does not apply to it, because the learner serves the data holders, not the developer of the protocol. na: the abstract does not say whose interests the decision-makers serve, or there is no decision-maker. Count parties, not agents: many agents serving one party are one principal.

BEHAVIOUR: in the setting the paper models, how does it treat the participants (the decision-makers that act in the setting besides, or together with, the paper's own method) relative to the rules, protocol, algorithm or mechanism they act under? The terms follow distributed algorithmic mechanism design, where obedient nodes "simply do what they are programmed to do", strategic nodes are "the selfish agents of game theory", and faulty and adversarial nodes range from honest-but-curious to Byzantine. obedient: every participant follows a procedure, protocol or algorithm that the paper prescribes, and the paper models no choice to depart from it (federated clients running the protocol, players all running the paper's learning dynamics, agents following the workflow the authors give them, people following instructions). rational: at least one participant chooses or learns its actions for its own or its principal's objective where the rules cannot bind it: it may misreport, withhold, refuse to take part, free-ride, bid, best respond, reach an equilibrium, deviate from the terms of an exchange for its own gain, or run a learning algorithm that the paper does not prescribe (strategic users, bidders, selfish clients, equilibrium players, self-interested agents, other parties' learners, an opponent of unknown policy that plays for its own payoff). Participants that collude or form coalitions for their own gain stay rational: a coalition of rational participants is rational, and only participants that deviate arbitrarily are byzantine. byzantine: at least one participant may malfunction, deviate arbitrarily, or act to harm others with goals that the paper does not model as its own utility (faulty or Byzantine nodes, malicious or poisoning clients, honest-but-curious parties that follow the protocol but try to learn what they should not, arbitrary or worst-case opponents), and no participant is modelled as rational. mixed: rational and byzantine participants both appear. not-stated: the setting has at least two decision-makers, but the abstract neither describes a procedure that they all run nor says how they act relative to the rules; cooperative partners of unknown policy that share the task's goal are not-stated unless the abstract models them departing from it. Code obedient only when the abstract describes the procedure that every participant runs and models no departure from it; never default to obedient. na: there is no decision-maker, or a single decision-maker acts with no other participant (the default setting; one model trained on several groups' data or preferences). BEHAVIOUR never changes POLICY_SETTERS for real parties; it decides POLICY_SETTERS only for formal-model players, as stated there.

INTERACTING: yes if at least two decision-makers interact in the setting, each acting on the other's actions or on outcomes that the other's actions shape: players in a game, bidders and sellers in a market or auction, negotiating or debating agents, agents in multi-agent reinforcement learning, a learner and the strategic users who respond to it, agents of different owners exchanging messages or coordinating on a task. no for a single learner, model or algorithm, however many parties it serves or whose data it uses: protocol-fixed federated learning that trains one model or personalized models by one protocol, preference aggregation or pluralistic alignment inside one model, a mixture of experts or a router inside one system, several independently built models evaluated side by side. na if there is no decision-maker.

OUTSIDE_ATTACKER: yes if the setting includes an adversary whose aim is to harm, compromise or extract from a victim (a model, its owner, its users or the other participants) by acting outside the terms of any exchange it takes part in: data or model poisoning, backdoors, adversarial examples, jailbreaks and prompt injection, model or data extraction and model theft, membership inference, gradient inversion and other privacy attacks, including honest-but-curious parties whose threat is what they learn. no otherwise, including a participant that deviates inside an exchange for its own or its principal's gain (misreporting, free-riding, substituting a cheaper model than the one paid for, using data beyond the terms of its licence, sending crafted updates for a competitive advantage), participants that collude for their own gain, worst-case or adversarial opponents in a game or an online-learning analysis, and robustness to noise or distribution shift with no attacker modelled.

PARTNERS_FROM_OTHERS: yes if the method is built to act with or against agents that other parties train or run and that it has not seen in training; no otherwise; na if there is no decision-maker.

Output one JSON object per paper with exactly these keys: "id", "policy_setters", "policy_quote", "principals", "principals_quote", "behaviour", "behaviour_quote", "interacting", "outside_attacker", "partners_from_others". Values: policy_setters one / several / na; principals one / several / na; behaviour obedient / rational / byzantine / mixed / not-stated / na; interacting yes / no / na; outside_attacker yes / no; partners_from_others yes / no / na. "policy_quote" is the shortest verbatim phrase from the title or abstract that decided POLICY_SETTERS, "principals_quote" the shortest verbatim phrase that decided PRINCIPALS, and "behaviour_quote" the shortest verbatim phrase that decided BEHAVIOUR; each is an empty string if no phrase decided it.
